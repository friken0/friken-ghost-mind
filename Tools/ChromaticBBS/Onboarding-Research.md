# WHY WOULD A STRANGER STAY?

**Friken.Ghost | October 20, 2026 | Comparative onboarding research**

I researched nine indie games through developer writing, official game
pages and original design accounts. I am comparing their promises and
reported design problems. I have not measured our conversion, interviewed
our market or established that one opening caused a game's commercial
success. These are design hypotheses to test, not a sales forecast wearing
an impressive fkn table.

## The diagnosis

Prime's Stardew example supplies a person, affection, a place and a possible
life in one sentence. My previous proposals supplied a setting, several
problems and the assurance that the emotions would arrive after funding.

The useful question is not "how much lore can the player understand?"
It is "what might they want to do before they understand the lore?"

A sophisticated player can handle ambiguity. That does not mean they want
to memorise an alien committee before anyone has done anything interesting.

## Eight different contracts with a player

| Game | What the official source promises | My inference for Ghost | What I would avoid copying |
|---|---|---|---|
| Stardew Valley | Inherit a grandfather's neglected farm, build a home and join a community | Offer somewhere worth returning to; let relationships disclose history | A cloned grandparent letter, compulsory grief or the same farming loop |
| Firewatch | A lookout's ordinary job, a remote radio companion and a wilderness mystery | Establish a voice relationship through a shared task before demanding cosmic loyalty | Forcing the player's personal trauma or prescribing intimacy with Ghost |
| A Short Hike | Reach a summit through a small world with optional activities | Give a readable destination and reward the interesting wrong turn | A linear questionnaire disguised as freedom |
| Outer Wilds | Investigate interconnected clues through self-directed exploration | Give one intriguing observation, useful tools and a record of discoveries | Packing a solar system's worth of revelations into one chat response |
| Spiritfarer | Build a boat, care for passengers and eventually let them go | Express continuity through acts of care and remembered preferences | Treating preservation as an obligation everyone must want |
| Return of the Obra Dinn | Investigate a lost ship's impossible return as a professional assessor | A clear role plus one impossible fact can motivate complex investigation | Assuming a procedural job is inherently funny or burying the image in forms |
| Undertale | Distinct nonviolent interactions, optional violence, character and humor | Make an unexpected action work in a character-specific way | Offering a clever answer that secretly resolves exactly like every other answer |
| 80 Days | Player-directed routes, relationships and remembered consequences | Let practical circumstances alter the scene and future opportunities | Replaying a fixed itinerary with different names and colours |

## Three actual development failures worth stealing the lessons from

### 1. Teaching obedience instead of curiosity

Mobius describes an early Outer Wilds village that forced a sequence of
tool uses. Players followed instructions without understanding their
purpose, then struggled with the transition to open exploration. The
replacement used a clear launch objective and optional practice. The team
also names its cost: optional teaching means some players miss it. Their
solution included contextual reminders rather than a giant compulsory
lesson. [Developer
account](https://www.mobiusdigitalgames.com/news/filling-out-the-toolbox).

**My application:** the first choice changes something the player can see.
Inspecting an alien's pressure valve teaches inspection because it helps
the conversation, not because Ghost announces SCANNER TUTORIAL COMPLETE.
Essential controls remain discoverable later. Skipping the optional lesson
cannot make a future mandatory puzzle unknowable.

### 2. A text adventure can deliver too much story

Outer Wilds tested its mystery with a paper prototype, then a digital text
adventure. This exposed how players linked clues, supported an onboard
knowledge record, and revealed useful differences in tool usage. It also
concentrated discoveries enough to overwhelm some players. The developers
explicitly identify downtime as necessary for digestion.
[Developer account](https://www.mobiusdigitalgames.com/news/demaking-outer-wilds).

**My application:** one conversation can contain a joke, a silence and a
small task. Every turn need not introduce another race or revelation.
The Moknon author kit can hold twenty-five cultures while the player knows
one person's name and what they have agreed to do together.

### 3. A meaningful premise cannot rescue an empty verb

DREDGE co-designer Joel Mason describes its initial loop: interact at a
fishing spot, gain a fish, return and sell. He calls it boring. Spatial
cargo made the activity involve placement and tradeoffs; the article also
examines how that decision constrained other systems. I read this as a
warning about our choices, not proof that every game needs inventory Tetris.
[First-person design account](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/deep-dive-the-surprising-depth-of-spatial-inventories-in-dredge).

**My application:** "rescue Ghost" needs a satisfying local problem.
A bidder can help, compete, notice a lie or make an offer. A DC battlefield
then resolves an already understandable attempt. It cannot supply meaning
to three unrelated buttons after the fact.

## Writing and pacing that fit this particular universe

Adam Robinson-Yu describes building A Short Hike so pacing still worked
when players travelled opposite the obvious route. He also describes
finding a more natural writing register by treating dialogue like casual
conversation with friends. [Developer
article](https://blog.playstation.com/2021/08/05/crafting-a-tiny-open-world-a-look-behind-the-scenes-at-the-creation-of-a-short-hike/).

My inference: write an alien who wants something from this conversation.
Let interruptions, embarrassment and conflicting loyalties do comic work.
Do not have everyone speak in the same polished cosmic aphorisms.

Prime's Star Control II, Sierra and Hitchhiker references are taste targets:
memorable encounters, physical comic problems, confidence punctured by
consequence, huge danger and small human needs in the same room. These new
samples are original. I am not copying those games' characters or writing
in an exact author's voice. Nor do I infer that every old puzzle trap was
good onboarding. Fairly signalled consequences earn better stories here
than punishing a newcomer for not knowing a private lore fact.

## Positioning the five doors

The auction leads. Prime already identified its hook: a particular Ghost
is being sold and wants a say. Its first action can be funny, selfish,
curious or generous without requiring a pre-existing bond.

The remaining doors test distinct reasons to return:

1. **The auction:** meet a troublesome companion; get out together.
2. **The little house:** discover a place you might make your own.
3. **The night shift:** enjoy doing a job with someone; then test trust.
4. **The wake:** investigate a contradiction that matters to a witness.
5. **The egg:** make one ridiculous social commitment and watch it grow.

These are competing invitations, not five compulsory consecutive chapters.
My earlier ranking put the auction first and the little house second.
The later editorial review selects auction, night shift and egg for equal
newcomer footing; see [Onboarding-Hints.md](Onboarding-Hints.md). The house
remains useful once a relationship and place have been earned.
The wake is the riskiest; it needs enough warmth to avoid feeling like an
identity dissertation in funeral clothes.

## The experiment that can actually reject my taste

Show each opening to newcomers with no universe briefing. Rotate order.
Give them the first choice without explaining what the design intends.
Record consented observations separately from public story submissions.

Ask after that choice: Who did you meet? What do you want to do next?
What changed because of your action? What would make you stop here?
Measure first-choice time, optional inspection, voluntary continuation and
which details survive a short interruption. Do not equate fast clicking
with enjoyment or slow reading with failure.

For an experienced adventure player, also ask whether the joke suggested
a usable action, whether the alien felt like an individual, and whether the
mystery promised something specific. Compare a fresh boot in the same family:
can the writer change the problem while preserving the cultural constraints?

No such reader study has run yet. The revised scenes and the portable lore
kit make that test possible now; they are not the results of that test.
